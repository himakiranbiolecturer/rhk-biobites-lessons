02

Respiratory organs and human air passage

Structure follows habitat, organisation and the need for a thin, moist exchange surface.

Body surface

Simple diffusion

Sponges, cnidarians and flatworms exchange gases across the general body surface.

Cutaneous

Moist cuticle or skin

Earthworms use their moist cuticle; frogs can also exchange gases through moist skin.

Tracheal

Air tubes

Insects use a branching tracheal system that takes atmospheric air towards tissues.

Branchial

Gills

Fishes and many aquatic arthropods and molluscs use vascularised gills.

Pulmonary

Lungs

Amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals use vascularised pulmonary surfaces.

Conducting part

External nostrils to terminal bronchioles. It transports, filters, humidifies and warms air; it is not the main diffusion surface.

Exchange part

Alveoli and their ducts. Here O₂ and CO₂ diffuse between alveolar air and blood.

Pleura and thorax

Double pleural membranes enclose fluid that reduces friction. The airtight thoracic chamber allows volume changes to move the lungs.

Compare their transport forms, then connect loading and unloading to local conditions.

07

Regulation and respiratory disorders

Know the control centres, dominant chemical signal and core NCERT definitions.

Respiratory rhythm centre

Located in the medulla; primarily responsible for generating and maintaining respiratory rhythm.

Pneumotaxic centre

Located in the pons; moderates the functions of the rhythm centre and can alter respiratory rate.

Chemical regulation

A chemosensitive area near the rhythm centre responds strongly to CO₂ and H⁺. Aortic and carotid receptors also signal changes.

NEET trap: CO₂ and H⁺ are major regulators of respiratory rhythm; oxygen has a comparatively insignificant role in normal rhythm regulation.

Asthma

Difficulty in breathing with wheezing due to inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles.

Emphysema

Chronic damage to alveolar walls reduces respiratory surface; cigarette smoking is a major cause.

Occupational disorders

Long exposure to dust can cause inflammation and fibrosis, reducing lung capacity; protective measures are essential.

08

High-yield NEET traps

Revise these distinctions before attempting the test.

Inspiration is active

Diaphragm and external intercostals contract; thoracic volume rises and intrapulmonary pressure falls.

Normal expiration is passive

Relaxation restores thoracic volume and raises intrapulmonary pressure slightly above atmosphere.

RV cannot be exhaled

Residual volume stays after forceful expiration and is included in FRC and TLC—not VC.

CO₂ diffuses efficiently

Its solubility is about 20–25 times that of O₂, so a smaller pressure gradient can drive exchange.

Hb curve is sigmoid

Alveolar conditions support loading; tissue conditions support dissociation and delivery.

Most CO₂ is bicarbonate

Not carbaminohaemoglobin: roughly 70% is transported as HCO₃⁻.

11

Last-minute revision

Open each checkpoint for a rapid oral review.

What produces inspiration?

Contraction of the diaphragm and external intercostals increases thoracic and pulmonary volume. Intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric pressure, so air moves inward.

Which capacities contain residual volume?

Functional residual capacity (ERV + RV) and total lung capacity (VC + RV). Vital capacity excludes RV.

What drives gas diffusion?

Mainly partial-pressure gradients, with solubility and membrane thickness also important. O₂ moves alveoli → blood → tissues; CO₂ moves tissues → blood → alveoli.

How are the two gases transported?

O₂: about 97% via RBC haemoglobin and 3% dissolved. CO₂: about 70% bicarbonate, 20–25% carbaminohaemoglobin and about 7% dissolved.
