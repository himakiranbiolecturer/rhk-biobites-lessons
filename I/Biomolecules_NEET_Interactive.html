Analysis

Elements, compounds & ash

Living and non-living samples contain broadly similar elements, but their relative abundance differs. Carbon and hydrogen are proportionally richer in living tissue.

  • Wet weight includes water; dry weight remains after drying.
  • Burning removes carbon compounds as gases.
  • Ash retains inorganic elements and salts.
  • Water is the most abundant cellular chemical.
Exam lens

Qualitative elemental presence can be similar; the biologically important difference is relative abundance.

Fractions

Soluble versus insoluble

Acid-soluble compounds are commonly about 18–800 Da. Proteins, polysaccharides and nucleic acids in the insoluble fraction are usually 10,000 Da or more.

  • Soluble pool approximates cytoplasmic composition.
  • Insoluble fraction includes cytoplasmic and organellar macromolecules.
  • Together they represent tissue chemistry.
Exam lens

Lipids are low-molecular-mass molecules but appear with the insoluble fraction because disrupted membranes form water-insoluble vesicles.

Amino acids

α-carbon and the R group

Protein amino acids carry amino, carboxyl, hydrogen and variable R groups on the same α-carbon. The R group gives each amino acid its character.

  • Glycine R = H; alanine R = methyl.
  • Acidic example: glutamic acid.
  • Basic example: lysine; neutral example: valine.
  • Ionisable groups allow a zwitterionic form.
Exam lens

Twenty amino-acid types occur in proteins; classification depends on R group and relative amino/carboxyl groups.

Lipids

Fatty acids, glycerol & phospholipids

A fatty acid contains a carboxyl group attached to a hydrocarbon R group. Glycerol is trihydroxy propane.

  • Saturated: no C=C; unsaturated: one or more C=C.
  • Mono-, di- and triglycerides reflect fatty-acid number on glycerol.
  • Oils have lower melting points than fats.
  • Phospholipids occur in membranes; lecithin is an example.
Exam lens

Palmitic acid has 16 carbons and arachidonic acid has 20, counting the carboxyl carbon.

Nucleotide basics

Base + sugar + phosphate

A nitrogenous base attached to a pentose sugar is a nucleoside. Addition of phosphate makes a nucleotide.

base + sugar = nucleoside
nucleoside + phosphate = nucleotide
  • Purines: adenine, guanine.
  • Pyrimidines: cytosine, thymine, uracil.
  • DNA has deoxyribose; RNA has ribose.
Exam lens

Adenosine is a nucleoside; adenylic acid is a nucleotide.

Metabolites

Primary & secondary

Primary metabolites take part in recognised normal physiological processes. Plants, fungi and microbes also produce diverse secondary metabolites.

  • Secondary examples: alkaloids, flavonoids, rubber and essential oils.
  • Also antibiotics, pigments, scents, gums and spices.
  • Many support human welfare or ecological interactions.
Exam lens

“Secondary” does not mean useless. It means not universally tied to the basic primary pathways.

Biomacromolecules

True polymers—and the lipid exception

Proteins, polysaccharides and nucleic acids are true polymeric macromolecules. Lipids enter the macromolecular fraction experimentally but are not strictly macromolecules.

  • Proteins: amino-acid polymers.
  • Polysaccharides: sugar polymers.
  • Nucleic acids: nucleotide polymers.
  • Lipids: membrane-associated small molecules.
Exam lens

Differentiate “found in the acid-insoluble fraction” from “is a polymeric macromolecule.”

Proteins

Heteropolymers with many roles

Proteins are polypeptides—linear amino-acid chains linked by peptide bonds. Because twenty monomer types can occur, proteins are heteropolymers.

  • Essential amino acids must come from diet.
  • Collagen: most abundant animal protein.
  • RuBisCO: most abundant protein in the biosphere.
  • Trypsin, insulin, antibodies, receptors and GLUT-4 illustrate varied roles.
Exam lens

Do not confuse “most abundant in animals” (collagen) with “most abundant in the biosphere” (RuBisCO).

Polysaccharides

Storage and structure

Long sugar chains may be homopolymers or complex polysaccharides.

  • Cellulose: glucose homopolymer; plant wall.
  • Starch: plant energy reserve; helical regions trap iodine and turn blue.
  • Glycogen: branched animal storage polysaccharide.
  • Inulin: fructose polymer; chitin occurs in arthropod exoskeleton.
Exam lens

Cellulose lacks the complex helix that holds iodine, so it does not give starch’s blue iodine test.

Nucleic acids

Polynucleotides

DNA and RNA are true macromolecular polymers made of nucleotides and serve as genetic material.

  • Nucleotide components: base, pentose, phosphate.
  • DNA contains 2′-deoxyribose.
  • RNA contains ribose.
  • A/G are purines; C/T/U are pyrimidines.
Exam lens

A nucleotide, not a nucleoside, is the repeating building block of nucleic acid.

Protein structure

Four organisational levels

Primary structure records amino-acid sequence from N- to C-terminal. Secondary structure includes local folds such as α-helix and β-pleated sheet.

  • Tertiary: overall 3D folding of one chain.
  • Quaternary: arrangement of multiple folded subunits.
  • Adult haemoglobin: two α + two β subunits.
  • Biological activity depends strongly on 3D structure.
Exam lens

A single polypeptide can have tertiary structure; quaternary structure requires more than one subunit.

Enzyme action

Active site and activation energy

Almost all enzymes are proteins; catalytic nucleic acids are ribozymes. Substrate binding forms a short-lived ES complex.

E + S ⇌ ES → EP → E + P
  • Active site is a pocket in the 3D enzyme.
  • Binding can tighten the enzyme around substrate.
  • Enzymes lower activation energy.
  • The enzyme emerges unchanged and can catalyse again.
Exam lens

Enzymes alter rate, not the energy difference between substrate and product.

Rate factors

Temperature, pH and substrate

Each enzyme has optimum temperature and pH. Activity declines on either side; high heat may denature the protein.

  • Low temperature often causes temporary inactivity.
  • Velocity initially rises with substrate concentration.
  • At Vmax, active sites are saturated.
  • Thermophile enzymes can remain stable at very high temperatures.
Exam lens

Adding substrate beyond saturation does not increase Vmax because free enzyme molecules are unavailable.

Inhibition & classes

Competitive inhibition

A structurally similar inhibitor competes with substrate for the active site. NCERT’s example is malonate inhibition of succinic dehydrogenase.

  • Oxidoreductases: redox.
  • Transferases: group transfer.
  • Hydrolases: hydrolysis.
  • Lyases: group removal without hydrolysis.
  • Isomerases: isomer conversion; ligases: joining.
Exam lens

Malonate resembles succinate—the substrate—not the product.

Cofactors

Apoenzyme + helper

Some enzymes require a non-protein cofactor. The protein portion alone is the apoenzyme.

  • Prosthetic group: tightly bound organic cofactor; haem is an example.
  • Coenzyme: transiently associated organic cofactor.
  • NAD/NADP contain niacin-derived components.
  • Metal ions coordinate with active-site groups and substrate.
  • Zinc assists carboxypeptidase.
Exam lens

Removing a required cofactor causes loss of catalytic activity.

Enzyme concept

Why catalysts speed reactions

The enzyme supplies a lower-energy route to the transition state; it does not change the starting or final energy levels.

Activation energy is the hill

Substrate must pass through a high-energy transition state whether the overall reaction releases or requires energy. Enzyme binding stabilises the route and lowers this barrier.

Without enzymeWith enzyme

Key rule: enzyme is released unchanged after product formation and can begin another catalytic cycle.

NEET traps

Sixteen corrections

Replace each tempting shortcut with the exact NCERT-aligned idea.

Elements are not unique

Living and non-living matter share elements; proportions differ.

Ash is inorganic

Burning removes oxidised carbon compounds.

Lipids are the exception

They enter the insoluble fraction but are not true macromolecules.

Secondary ≠ useless

Many have ecological and human value.

Amino acid count

Twenty types are used in proteins.

Saturated means no C=C

Unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more double bonds.

Nucleoside lacks phosphate

Add phosphate to form a nucleotide.

Protein = heteropolymer

It can use many amino-acid monomer types.

Abundance distinction

Collagen: animals; RuBisCO: entire biosphere.

Iodine fits starch helix

Cellulose lacks the complex iodine-holding helix.

Inulin monomer

Inulin is a fructose polymer.

Quaternary needs subunits

Tertiary structure can occur in a single chain.

Not every enzyme is protein

Ribozymes are catalytic nucleic acids.

Enzyme lowers Eₐ

It does not change substrate/product energy difference.

Vmax is saturation

Extra substrate cannot create more free active sites.

Malonate competes

It resembles succinate and occupies the binding site.
