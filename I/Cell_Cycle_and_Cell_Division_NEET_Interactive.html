Cell cycle

Coordinated growth and division

The cell cycle is the ordered sequence in which a cell duplicates its genome, synthesises cellular components and divides into daughter cells.

  • Cell growth is continuous.
  • DNA synthesis occurs only during S phase.
  • Interphase lies between successive M phases.
  • Human cultured cells average about 24 h; yeast may finish in ~90 min.
Exam lens

Interphase is metabolically active, not a truly resting state, and occupies more than 95% of the average human-cell cycle.

Interphase

G₁ → S → G₂

G₁ supports metabolism and growth without DNA replication. S duplicates DNA; G₂ continues growth and makes proteins for mitosis.

  • S phase: DNA 2C → 4C.
  • Chromosome number remains 2n after S.
  • Animal centriole duplicates during S.
  • G₂ prepares mitotic machinery.
Exam lens

DNA content doubles in S, but chromosome count does not because sister chromatids remain joined at one centromere.

G₀

Quiescent but active

Cells may leave G₁ and enter G₀ when they do not divide further or divide only when required.

  • G₀ cells remain metabolically active.
  • They do not proliferate unless called upon.
  • Adult heart cells are a cited non-dividing example.
Exam lens

Quiescence means absence of proliferation, not metabolic inactivity.

Prophase

Condense and organise

Chromatin condenses into distinct two-chromatid chromosomes. Duplicated centrosomes move apart and organise asters and spindle fibres.

  • Mitotic apparatus = two asters + spindle fibres.
  • Nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear by late prophase.
  • Golgi complex and ER are not visible at the end of prophase.
Exam lens

Prophase follows S and G₂; DNA is already replicated before chromosome condensation begins.

Metaphase

Alignment at the plate

Fully condensed chromosomes align at the equator. Opposite spindle poles attach to sister-chromatid kinetochores.

  • Nuclear envelope has disintegrated.
  • Chromosome morphology is easiest to study.
  • Alignment plane = metaphase plate.
  • Kinetochores are disc-shaped centromere structures.
Exam lens

The centromere holds chromatids; the kinetochore is the spindle-attachment site.

Anaphase

Centromeres split

Sister chromatids separate simultaneously and become daughter chromosomes moving to opposite poles.

  • Centromere leads toward the pole.
  • Chromosome arms trail behind.
  • Each future nucleus receives an identical set.
Exam lens

Mitotic anaphase separates sister chromatids; anaphase I separates homologous chromosomes without centromere division.

Telophase & cytokinesis

Rebuild and separate

Chromosomes decondense at opposite poles. Nuclear envelope, nucleolus, Golgi and ER re-form; cytoplasm then divides.

  • Animal cell: cleavage furrow grows inward.
  • Plant cell: cell plate begins centrally and grows outward.
  • Cell plate forms the future middle lamella.
  • No cytokinesis after karyokinesis can produce a syncytium.
Exam lens

Liquid coconut endosperm is the NCERT example of a multinucleate syncytium.

Mitosis

Why equational division matters

Mitosis generally preserves chromosome number and produces genetically similar daughter cells.

  • Growth of multicellular organisms.
  • Replacement of epidermal, gut-lining and blood cells.
  • Cell repair.
  • Restoration of nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio.
  • Plant growth via apical and lateral cambium.
Exam lens

Mitosis occurs in haploid male honey-bee cells and in both haploid and diploid plant stages—important exceptions to the usual diploid rule.

Meiosis overview

One replication, two divisions

Meiosis begins after S phase and produces four haploid cells through meiosis I and II.

  • Meiosis I: reductional; homologues separate.
  • Meiosis II: equational; sister chromatids separate.
  • Homologues pair and recombine.
  • No DNA replication during interkinesis.
Exam lens

Two nuclear divisions do not mean two S phases; DNA replicates only once before meiosis I.

Prophase I

Leptotene to diakinesis

Leptotene→Zygotene→Pachytene→Diplotene→Diakinesis
  • Leptotene: chromosomes become visible.
  • Zygotene: synapsis and synaptonemal complex.
  • Pachytene: tetrads, recombination nodules, crossing over.
  • Diplotene: chiasmata visible as homologues separate.
  • Diakinesis: terminalisation and full condensation.
Exam lens

Crossing over occurs in pachytene; chiasmata become evident in diplotene; terminalisation occurs in diakinesis.

Pairing & recombination

Bivalent, tetrad and chiasma

Synapsed homologous chromosomes form a bivalent. Its four chromatids make a tetrad.

  • Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids.
  • Recombination nodules mark crossover sites.
  • Recombinase mediates crossing over.
  • Chiasmata are X-shaped crossover connections visible later.
Exam lens

Bivalent counts chromosomes (two homologues); tetrad highlights chromatids (four).

Meiosis I

Reductional separation

Bivalents align at metaphase I. Homologous chromosomes then move to opposite poles while sister chromatids remain joined.

  • Metaphase I: homologous kinetochores attach to opposite poles.
  • Anaphase I: centromeres do not split.
  • Telophase I and cytokinesis may form a dyad.
  • Interkinesis is short and has no S phase.
Exam lens

After meiosis I, each cell is haploid but each chromosome still has two sister chromatids.

Meiosis II

A mitosis-like division

Chromosomes realign singly at metaphase II. Centromeres split at anaphase II, allowing sister chromatids to separate.

  • Prophase II is simpler than prophase I.
  • Opposite poles attach to sister kinetochores.
  • Telophase II encloses new haploid nuclei.
  • Cytokinesis yields a tetrad of four haploid cells.
Exam lens

Meiosis II does not reduce n further; it reduces DNA content per cell by separating sister chromatids.

Meiosis

Constancy plus variation

Meiosis generates haploid cells so fertilisation can restore diploidy without doubling chromosome number each generation.

  • Conserves species-specific chromosome number.
  • Crossing over creates new allele combinations.
  • Segregation increases genetic variability.
  • Variation supplies material for evolution.
Exam lens

Meiosis halves chromosome number in one generation yet helps preserve the species number across generations.

NEET traps

Sixteen corrections

Each card replaces a tempting shortcut with the precise NCERT distinction.

Interphase ≠ rest

It is metabolically active and prepares for division.

S phase: C changes, n does not

DNA doubles while joined chromatids keep chromosome count constant.

G₀ is active

Cells stop proliferating, not metabolism.

Kinetochore ≠ centromere

Kinetochore is the spindle-attachment structure on centromere.

Study morphology in metaphase

Chromosome condensation is complete and structures are clear.

Anaphase chromosome count

Separated chromatids are counted as daughter chromosomes.

Plant plate grows outward

Animal furrow grows inward.

Syncytium

Karyokinesis without cytokinesis produces multinucleate cytoplasm.

One S phase, two meiotic divisions

Interkinesis has no DNA replication.

Synapsis in zygotene

Do not shift it to pachytene.

Crossing over in pachytene

Recombination nodules appear here.

Chiasmata in diplotene

They become visible as the complex dissolves.

Terminalisation in diakinesis

It is the final prophase-I stage.

Anaphase I preserves centromeres

Homologues separate; sisters remain together.

Meiosis II is equational

It separates sister chromatids without further halving n.

Honey-bee exception

Haploid male cells can divide mitotically.
